The Product

For a generation, the promise of technology in medicine was to make doctors and nurses more effective. By 2025, that promise has been broken. Clinicians are now buried under a mountain of clicks, alerts, and after-hours charting, leaving them burned out and disconnected from their patients.

I took on the challenge of designing an Electronic Health Record (EHR) experience that brings the focus back to what matters: the human side of medicine.

The Problem

For more than a decade, the American healthcare system has invested tens of billions of dollars in the arduous task of digitizing patient records and, thus, building an EHR ecosystem. However, the experience from the side of healthcare professionals is not what was expected. Studies show that doctors spend about half of their workday on EHR applications and desktop tasks and less than a third of their time in direct patient interaction. This is compounded by an additional 1-2 hours of work documenting after their shift. There are multiple reports confirming that clinical staff now spend up to two hours on administrative or EHR tasks for every hour of face-to-face patient care.

The Goal

My goal for the project was to restore humanity to the clinical encounter. The premise was simple: to make the encounter between patient and caregiver a conversation by making technology a helper, not the violinist on a two-person date.

Our high-level objectives were:

  1. To remove the screen as a barrier.
  2. To create an intuitive system that felt like an assistant, not an obstacle.

Summary

Gaining access to the insights of practicing physicians and nurses in US clinics for this student project was a challenge that I was able to overcome by conducting a solid research process, based on the in-depth analysis of existing industry data.

Initially, I assumed that those who were dissatisfied with the EHRs were the doctors with more years of experience. However, the research showed me that the frustration was universal; even the youngest staff were driven to inefficiency, such as paper-based workarounds due to poorly designed software.

My conclusions were based on a review of industry research from sources such as the KLAS Arch Collaborative and qualitative studies on nursing workflows, which indicate that EHR usability is a primary driver of burnout across all clinician age groups and that paper-based workarounds are a common, rational response to inefficient digital processes. Pain points

In my research, I have found three major pain points:

  • Inefficient and Redundant Workflows: Nurses are forced into a process of "double-charting"—capturing patient information on paper in the exam room and then manually re-entering it into a desktop computer later.
  • High Cognitive Load & Information Overload: Physicians are presented with cluttered, data-dense screens that make it difficult to quickly find the specific information they need during a time-pressed patient visit.
  • The Screen as a Barrier to Connection: The clumsy, desktop-based design of current systems forces physicians to turn their backs on their patients to interact with the software.

Based on these three fundamental findings, I developed 4 user personas that allowed me to understand the daily difficulties they faced. The first user person is Maria Sanchez, a busy front-line nurse focused on accuracy and efficiency who needs a faster and more reliable way to chart the patient's vitals in the exam room because to chart twice is a waste of time and it has risk errors. The second user person is Linda Carter, a time-pressed physician who prioritizes patient connection who needs a see a one-glance summary of the patient's problems and medications when I open their chart because searching through all documentation means less time for the patient attention. The third user person is Robert Miller, a senior physician who is easily overwhelmed by complex technology. He needs an easier way to navigate through the EHR of each patient and an easier way to save patient's health data because that consumes an important amount of time from him and any available nurse that helps him. And the fourth is Jessica Willias, a specialized nurse who needs a way to automatically see a graph data of the patience's health over time so that she can instantly identify trends because to waste time searching data and identifying trends may prevent the patient from receiving enough time and education.

Then I proceeded to establish their current user journey map, and with each one I was able to verify the problem of María's double charting, which led her to feel frustrated; or Dr. Carter's constant fatigue, who took her to pajama hours even though the system was supposed to help her perform better in her profession.

Next I present my detailed work on both the user personas created and their respective user journey maps:

Maria Sanchez

A busy front-line nurse focused on accuracy and efficiency

Maria Sanchez

“I just want to get the information in correctly on the first try so I can get back to my patients.”

Age: 28

Education: Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN)

Hometown: Austin, TX

Family: Single, lives downtown with a roommate.

Occupation: Nurse

Goals

  • To chart patient vitals and notes in real-time while in the exam room.
  • To avoid the time-wasting process of "double-charting" on paper first, then on the computer.
  • To have a patient's allergies and critical alerts immediately visible.
  • To send quick, reliable updates to doctors without having to physically find them.

Frustrations

  • The clinic's Wi-Fi is spotty in exam rooms, making mobile charting impossible.
  • There is always a line to use the slow desktop computers at the nursing station.
  • The current EHR requires too many clicks and screens to enter simple data.
  • She wastes the last hour of every shift just catching up on documentation.

Maria is constantly on her feet, moving between exam rooms, the supply closet, and the central nursing station. Her workflow is a fragmented loop: she jots down a patient's vitals and symptoms on a notepad in the room, then rushes back to a shared computer to enter the same data into the slow system, all while trying to prepare for her next patient.

Linda Carter

A time-pressed physician who prioritizes patient connection

Dr. Linda Carter

“I spend more time making eye contact with my computer screen than with my patients, and that's not why I became a doctor.”

Age: 37

Education: Doctor of Medicine (MD), Board Certified in Family Medicine

Hometown: Santa Ana, California

Family: Married with two children.

Occupation: Physician

Goals

  • To see a quick, one-glance summary of a patient's active problems and medications.
  • To maintain eye contact and natural conversation with patients during visits.
  • To eliminate the 1-2 hours of "pajama time" she spends finishing charts at home every night.
  • To trust that the information in the chart, like medication lists, is always up-to-date and accurate.

Frustrations

  • The system bombards her with constant, meaningless pop-up alerts ("alert fatigue").
  • The cluttered interface makes it hard to quickly find the specific piece of information he needs.
  • Simple tasks, like prescribing a medication or ordering a lab test, require navigating a maze of clicks.
  • The system's design forces her to turn her back on the patient, breaking the human connection.

Dr. Carter's day is a series of back-to-back 15-minute appointments. She enters each exam room feeling torn between greeting his patient and battling the cumbersome EHR on the desktop in the corner. She attempts to type her notes while conversing, but feels she is failing at both, leading to long evenings at home correcting and completing her documentation for the day.

Robert Miller

A senior physician who is easily overwhelmed by complex technology

Dr. Robert Miller

“I just want the computer to get out of the way so I can focus on what I've done for 40 years: practice medicine”

Age: 67

Education: Doctor of Medicine (MD), Board Certified in Internal Medicine

Hometown: Lexington-Fayette, Kentucky

Family: Widower, with adult children.

Occupation: Physician.

Goals

  • To see a simple, clean interface with fewer buttons and options.
  • To enter notes efficiently using voice dictation rather than typing.
  • To find basic information (like the reason for today's visit) without getting lost in confusing tabs.
  • To feel confident and in control when using the clinic's technology.

Frustrations

  • The system is visually cluttered and overwhelming, causing "information overload."
  • He can't focus on the patient's story and navigate the complex software at the same time.
  • He frequently gets lost in the interface and needs to ask younger staff for help.
  • Using the current EHR makes him feel slow, incompetent, and adds hours to his workday.

Dr. Miller's day is a constant struggle against the technology meant to help him. He makes a point to speak with his patients first, taking sparse notes on a pad. When he turns to the computer, he navigates slowly, often getting lost in the maze of tabs and buttons. He feels a deep sense of frustration and inadequacy, frequently having to ask a nurse for help finding information or spending long hours after his shift dictating his notes for someone else to type in.

Jessica Williams

A specialized nurse who needs a way to see an automatic graph of the patient's health data

Jessica Williams

“The data is all in there somewhere, but the system can't show me the one thing I need to see: the story over time.”

Age: 47

Education: Associate Degree in Nursing (ADN), Certified Diabetes Care

Hometown: Buffalo, New York

Family: Married, one son in college.

Occupation: Nurse.

Goals

  • To see trends in a patient's key health data (e.g., blood sugar, weight, BP) on a single, graphical screen.
  • To spend less time hunting for data and more time using it to educate patients.
  • To easily share clear, visual data with patients to help them understand their condition.
  • To track the clinic's patient flow and resource usage from a central dashboard.

Frustrations

  • The system presents vital data as fragmented lists instead of as an integrated trend or graph.
  • She has to manually build flowsheets on paper before each specialized patient visit.
  • There's no way to see a high-level, real-time view of the clinic's operational status.
  • The system cannot integrate data from patients' modern home health devices.

Before each specialized patient consultation, Jessica spends 20 minutes acting like a detective. She digs through multiple tabs in the EHR to find and manually write down key data points on a paper flowsheet. Only with this self-made tool can she see the "big picture" and effectively review health trends with her patients, which is the primary function of her job.

Conclusion

This process allowed me to understand the difficulties that nurses and doctors face. This is how I learned that they need a true system that makes their profession easier, so that they can dedicate all their talent to what is most important: human contact with the patient.
